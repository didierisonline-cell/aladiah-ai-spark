## Lesson 1 · The BA Competency Map
**Competency:** `ba:requirements` · **Output:** Competency Self-Assessment

### Objectives
- Map the BABOK knowledge areas onto Aladiah's 13-competency model.
- Score yourself honestly across all 13 competencies against your target role.
- Produce a Competency Self-Assessment that targets your effort for the rest of the program.

### Lesson
In Module 1 you chose a target role on the salary ladder. This lesson tells you *exactly* what
competencies that role requires — so your effort is aimed, not scattered.

The profession's reference body of knowledge, **IIBA's BABOK**, organizes BA work into knowledge
areas: business-analysis planning, elicitation & collaboration, requirements lifecycle management,
strategy analysis, requirements analysis & design, and solution evaluation. BABOK is excellent — and
incomplete for 2026, because it predates AI-native practice and modern product discovery. Aladiah's
**13-competency model** keeps everything BABOK covers and adds what the market now pays for:
`product-discovery`, `product-thinking`, `business-architecture`, `ai-analysis`, `ai-prompting`, and
`compliance` at depth.

Here's the key insight: **different roles light up different competencies.** A *Junior BA* leans on
`requirements`, `elicitation`, `process-analysis`. A *Product Analyst* adds `product-thinking` and
`product-discovery`. A *Transformation Consultant* is defined by `business-architecture`,
`stakeholders`, and `solution-eval`. Knowing which competencies your target role demands tells you
which modules matter most for *you* — and which portfolio artifacts will most move your career.

Real example: two analysts complete this program. One is targeting Senior BA and invests deepest in
requirements, process, and data; the other is targeting Transformation Consultant and invests deepest
in architecture, discovery, and stakeholder strategy. Same curriculum, different emphasis, different
destinations — both deliberate.

### Practical exercise
Score yourself 1–5 on each of the 13 competencies (be honest — this is for you). Mark the 4–5
competencies your **target role** most requires. The gap between your scores and your target is your
personal syllabus.

### Artifact produced — **Competency Self-Assessment**
A 13-competency radar (current vs. target) with your top 3 gaps named — revisited at the capstone to
show measurable growth.

## Lesson 2 · Planning the Analysis Approach
**Competency:** `ba:requirements` · **Output:** BA Plan

### Objectives
- Plan how you'll do analysis on an initiative: scope, stakeholders, communication, governance.
- Choose a predictive, adaptive, or hybrid approach and justify it.
- Produce a reusable BA Plan.

### Lesson
Amateurs start analysis by "gathering requirements." Professionals start by **planning the analysis
itself** — because how you'll work depends entirely on context, and getting that wrong wastes weeks.

A BA Plan answers, up front: *Who are the stakeholders and how will I engage them? How will I capture
and manage requirements? What's the governance — who approves what? How will information be stored and
traced? What's my approach — predictive, adaptive, or hybrid?*

That last choice matters most. **Predictive (plan-driven)** analysis suits stable, heavily-regulated,
fixed-scope work — e.g., a core-banking compliance change where requirements must be specified and
signed off before build. **Adaptive (agile)** analysis suits uncertain, evolving product work —
continuous discovery, just-in-time requirements, learning as you go. Most real initiatives are
**hybrid**: an agile product built inside a regulated enterprise needs adaptive discovery *and*
predictive compliance gates. A senior BA names the approach deliberately and tailors the plan to it,
rather than applying one rigid template to everything.

Real example: the same analyst plans two initiatives differently — a GDPR data-retention change (heavy
governance, formal sign-offs, full traceability) and a new returns experience (lightweight, iterative,
discovery-led). Using the regulated plan on the product work would smother it; using the product plan
on the regulated work would fail the audit. Matching approach to context is the skill.

### Practical exercise
For an initiative you know, draft a one-page BA Plan: stakeholders, engagement cadence, requirements
approach, governance/sign-offs, and predictive/adaptive/hybrid — with one sentence justifying the choice.

### Artifact produced — **BA Plan**
A reusable one-page plan template you'll apply (and adapt) to every engagement, including the capstone.

## Lesson 3 · The Modern BA AI Toolkit
**Competency:** `ba:ai-analysis` · **Output:** Tool Selection Matrix

### Objectives
- Map AI tools to each stage of the BA workflow.
- Classify each tool by operating model (assistive / generative / agentic) and the validation it demands.
- Produce a Tool Selection Matrix.

### Lesson
In Module 1 you learned the AI **operating-model spectrum** (assistive → generative → agentic). This
lesson applies it to your actual toolkit, stage by stage:
- **Capture** — interview transcription (Otter, Fireflies, native meeting transcription): *assistive*;
  validation is light (fix transcription errors).
- **Synthesis** — summarizing interviews, clustering findings (Claude, ChatGPT): *generative*;
  validation is heavy — trace each theme to evidence, check for omitted dissent (your Module-1 loop).
- **Drafting** — requirements, user stories, acceptance criteria (Claude, Copilot): *generative*;
  validate against business rules and the AI Requirement Ladder.
- **Modeling** — diagramming and process maps (Lucid AI, Miro AI): *generative*; validate the logic
  against the real process with stakeholders.
- **Grounded research** — querying your own documents with citations (NotebookLM, Microsoft 365
  Copilot): *generative-grounded*; validate the citations are real and correctly used.
- **Data** — natural-language-to-SQL and analysis copilots: *generative*; validate the query logic and
  the result.

The discipline is not "use AI everywhere." It's: for each task, pick the tool, know its operating
model, and apply the *right weight* of validation. A BA who can produce this mapping works visibly
faster and safer than one improvising with whatever's open in a browser tab.

Real example: a BA runs eight discovery interviews, uses transcription to capture them (assistive,
light check), then an LLM to cluster themes (generative, heavy validation against the transcripts) —
turning two days of manual synthesis into two hours, *with* an evidence trail. Same output a week
faster, and defensible.

### Practical exercise
List the stages of a real piece of BA work. For each, name the AI tool you'd use, its operating model,
and the one validation check you'd run. Notice where validation must be heaviest.

### Artifact produced — **Tool Selection Matrix**
A table: BA task → tool → operating model (assistive/generative/agentic) → required validation. Your
personal, defensible AI workflow.

## Lesson 4 · Working with AI Responsibly
**Competency:** `ba:ai-analysis` · **Output:** AI Usage Guardrails

### Objectives
- Identify the real risks of using AI in BA work (data, accuracy, disclosure).
- Set personal guardrails for when and how to use AI — and when not to.
- Produce a personal AI Usage Guardrails policy.

### Lesson
Module 1 showed how AI *systems* fail without governance (COMPAS, the Obermeyer algorithm). This
lesson is about how *you*, the BA, use AI responsibly in your own work — a more immediate risk.

Three guardrails matter most:
1. **Data privacy — the one that gets people fired.** Pasting confidential interview transcripts,
   customer PII, or proprietary documents into a *public* AI tool can breach GDPR/HIPAA/contracts and
   leak data into a third party. Real pattern: Samsung engineers pasted proprietary source code into
   ChatGPT in 2023, leaking internal secrets — prompting an outright ban. The BA rule: use
   **approved/enterprise** tools with data-handling guarantees, or **de-identify** data first. Never
   feed confidential data to an unapproved tool for convenience.
2. **Accuracy & validation.** Treat every AI output as a draft to verify against sources (your
   Module-1 operating model). Confident, fluent, and *wrong* is AI's default failure mode.
3. **Disclosure & auditability.** Flag AI-assisted work where it matters, and keep a record of what
   was AI-generated and who reviewed it — so the work is defensible at audit.

And the underrated skill: **knowing when *not* to use AI.** High-stakes judgment, sensitive
stakeholder dynamics, and novel strategic calls are human work. The mature BA uses AI to remove the
mechanical load so they can spend *more* time on exactly the judgment AI can't do.

### Practical exercise
Write three personal rules: (1) what data you will never put in a public tool, (2) how you'll validate
AI output before using it, (3) one situation where you'll deliberately not use AI. Keep it to a card.

### Artifact produced — **AI Usage Guardrails**
A short personal policy (data rules · validation rule · human-only situations) you can show an
employer to prove you use AI responsibly — increasingly a hiring question.

## Lesson 5 · Communication & Collaboration Foundations
**Competency:** `ba:facilitation` · **Output:** Communication Charter

### Objectives
- Treat communication as a core BA competency, not a soft skill.
- Tailor the same message to executives, engineers, data scientists, and product partners.
- Produce a Communication Charter for how you'll work with a team.

### Lesson
A BA is a **business translator** — the person who makes the executive, the engineer, the data
scientist, and the customer understand each other. Brilliant analysis communicated badly is worth
nothing; this is where many technically-strong analysts stall (and why we make it explicit early).

The core skill is **audience tailoring** — the same finding, framed for who's listening:
- *To a CFO:* "This reduces refund-processing cost ~18% and pays back in two quarters." (Outcome,
  money, timeframe.)
- *To an engineer:* "We need an idempotent refund service with an audit log and a confidence
  threshold for auto-approval." (Precision, constraints.)
- *To a data scientist:* "We need ≥95% precision on auto-approvals and parity across regions."
  (Metrics, fairness.)
- *To a product partner:* "The real outcome is faster refunds; the portal is one option — let's
  validate." (Outcomes over outputs — straight from Module 1.)

Two more foundations: **active listening** (in elicitation, the goal is to understand, not to wait
for your turn — Module 4 goes deep) and **collaboration norms** for modern, often distributed and
global teams — async written clarity, decision logs, and respect across time zones and cultures
(directly relevant to Aladiah's worldwide students working with worldwide teams).

Real example: an analyst presents the *same* recommendation twice — a metrics-and-money version to the
steering committee that gets it funded, and a constraints-and-acceptance version to the dev team that
gets it built correctly. One analysis, two translations, both land. That is the competency.

### Practical exercise
Take one finding. Write it four ways — for a CFO, an engineer, a data scientist, and a product partner.
Notice what each audience needs and what they ignore.

### Artifact produced — **Communication Charter**
How you'll communicate on a team: audience map, cadence, channels, decision-logging, and collaboration
norms — the foundation for Module 3 (Stakeholders) and Module 5 (Facilitation).
