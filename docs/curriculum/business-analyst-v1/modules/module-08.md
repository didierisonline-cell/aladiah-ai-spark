## Lesson 1 · Capability Mapping
**Competency:** `ba:business-architecture` · **Output:** Capability Map

### Objectives
- Explain what a business capability is and why it's the most stable lens in the enterprise.
- Build a leveled capability map of a business domain.
- Produce a Capability Map.

### Lesson
A **business capability** is *what* a business is able to do — "Customer Onboarding," "Order
Management," "Fraud Detection," "Pricing" — described independently of *how* it's done, *who* does it,
or *what tools* are used. That independence is the point: org charts reorganize, processes change, and
systems get replaced, but capabilities are remarkably **stable**. That makes a **capability map** the
enterprise's most durable backbone for analysis, planning, and transformation — the discipline at the
heart of business architecture (the Business Architecture Guild's **BIZBOK**).

Capability maps are **leveled**: Level 1 is the handful of major capabilities ("Customer Management"),
Level 2 decomposes each ("Acquire Customer," "Onboard Customer," "Service Customer"), Level 3 goes
deeper where it matters. The skill is decomposing cleanly — each capability is a distinct *ability*,
named as a noun-ish "what," with no overlaps and no process verbs sneaking in. A good map lets an
executive see the *whole* business on one page, which is exactly why executives engage with it when
they'll ignore a process diagram.

Why it matters for transformation: capabilities become the **common language** that connects strategy
to execution. "We need to improve customer retention" is a strategy; it maps to specific capabilities
("Customer Service," "Loyalty Management," "Churn Prediction") that you can then assess and invest in.
Real example: a bank with dozens of overlapping "digital" projects had no way to prioritize them. A
capability map let leadership see that twelve projects all touched three capabilities — and that the
*strategically critical* capability was barely funded. The map turned project chaos into a portfolio
decision. That re-framing is what business architects are paid for.

### Practical exercise
Build a Level-1 + Level-2 capability map for an organization you know (8–15 capabilities). Check each
is a stable "what," not a process or a department.

### Artifact produced — **Capability Map** (showcaseable)
A clean, leveled capability map of a real business domain — a recognizable, executive-grade business-
architecture artifact that very few BAs can produce and that signals enterprise-level thinking.

## Lesson 2 · Value Streams & Operating Models
**Competency:** `ba:business-architecture` · **Output:** Operating Model Canvas

### Objectives
- Connect value streams (end-to-end value) to the capabilities that enable them.
- Describe an operating model — how the enterprise is configured to deliver strategy.
- Produce an Operating Model Canvas.

### Lesson
Capabilities are the *what*; **value streams** are the *flow* — the end-to-end stages that deliver value
to a customer or stakeholder ("Acquire-to-Onboard," "Order-to-Cash," "Issue-to-Resolution"). Business
architecture links the two: each value-stream stage is **enabled by capabilities**. This cross-mapping
is powerful — it shows which capabilities are load-bearing for the value customers actually experience,
so investment targets what matters to outcomes rather than what's loudest internally.

An **operating model** is the bigger answer to *how* the enterprise delivers its strategy: the
configuration of **people, process, technology, structure, governance, and information.** Strategy says
*what we want to achieve and where we'll play*; the operating model is *how we're organized to do it* —
the bridge between strategy and execution. Consultancies formalize the future version as a **Target
Operating Model (TOM)**. A BA who can read and shape the operating model is operating at the level that
redesigns how a business runs, not just what a team builds.

Real example: a retailer's strategy was "win omnichannel," but its operating model was two separate
org silos (stores vs. e-commerce) with separate systems, incentives, and P&Ls — structurally incapable
of delivering one seamless customer experience. No feature could fix a *structural* misalignment. The
business-architecture insight — strategy and operating model were contradicting each other — reframed
the whole transformation from "build a portal" to "redesign the operating model." That's the altitude
shift this module is about.

### Practical exercise
For a real organization, map one value stream to the capabilities that enable it. Then sketch its
operating model on a canvas (people · process · technology · structure · governance) and flag one place
where the operating model contradicts the strategy.

### Artifact produced — **Operating Model Canvas** (showcaseable)
A value-stream-to-capability mapping plus an operating-model canvas with a strategy-alignment flag — an
artifact that demonstrates you can analyze *how a business is structured to win*, not just its features.

> **Gate:** capability = value streams + operating models · artifact = Operating Model Canvas · recruiter ✅ · public ✅ · interview ✅.

## Lesson 3 · Capability Heatmaps & Investment
**Competency:** `ba:business-architecture` · **Output:** Capability Heatmap

### Objectives
- Assess capabilities on maturity, performance, and strategic importance.
- Use a heatmap to direct transformation investment where it pays off.
- Produce a Capability Heatmap.

### Lesson
A capability map shows *what* the business does; a **capability heatmap** shows *where to act.* You
overlay each capability with two reads — **strategic importance** (how much does winning the strategy
depend on this capability?) and **current maturity/performance** (how good are we at it today?) — and
color the map. The pattern that demands action jumps out instantly: **high importance + low maturity**
capabilities are your transformation priorities; high-maturity/low-importance areas may be
over-invested. The heatmap turns a sprawling enterprise into a small number of defensible investment
decisions.

This is precisely how top consultancies frame transformation: not "let's modernize everything" (which
burns budget across the board) but "these three capabilities are strategically critical and weak —
invest here, and leave the strong/unimportant ones alone." It makes the investment case **legible to
executives**, because it ties money to strategy through capability, not through technology fashion.

Real example: a firm wanted to "do AI everywhere." A capability heatmap showed its two strategically
critical capabilities — fraud detection and demand forecasting — were exactly the weak ones, while the
flashy customer-facing capability everyone wanted to AI-ify was already strong. The heatmap redirected
the AI budget to where it would move the strategy, and quietly killed several vanity projects. One
diagram, a year of misallocation avoided.

### Practical exercise
Take your capability map. Rate each capability on strategic importance (H/M/L) and maturity (H/M/L), and
color it. Circle the high-importance/low-maturity capabilities — your investment priorities.

### Artifact produced — **Capability Heatmap** (showcaseable)
A heatmapped capability map (importance × maturity) with investment priorities marked — a board-level
artifact that shows you can turn enterprise complexity into a clear, defensible investment decision.

> **Gate:** capability = capability assessment + investment prioritization · artifact = Capability Heatmap · recruiter ✅ · public ✅ · interview ✅.

## Lesson 4 · Target-State Architecture
**Competency:** `ba:business-architecture` · **Output:** Target-State Architecture

### Objectives
- Design a coherent future-state business architecture aligned to strategy.
- Define the gap between current and target state.
- Produce a Target-State Architecture.

### Lesson
The **target-state (to-be) business architecture** describes the future configuration of capabilities,
value streams, and operating model that *realizes the strategy* — the destination. It's not a fantasy
wish-list; it's a designed, coherent end-state where the pieces fit: the strategy implies certain
capabilities must become strong, which implies operating-model changes (structure, governance,
technology, skills), which implies a sequence to get there. Designing it is a synthesis of everything in
this module — and, like any to-be (Module 7), it's a **decision to be agreed with executives**, not an
analyst's private blueprint.

The other half is the **gap**: target minus current. The gap analysis — which capabilities must be
built or uplifted, which operating-model changes are required, which dependencies and risks exist — is
what a transformation *roadmap* is built from (Module 12's territory and the next module's blueprint).
A target state without an honest gap analysis is a poster; a target state *with* one is a plan.

Real example: a healthcare provider's strategy was "shift from volume to value-based care." The
target-state architecture made the implications concrete: new capabilities (population health
management, outcomes analytics), operating-model changes (care teams reorganized around patient
cohorts, new incentive structures), and a multi-year capability-uplift sequence. The gap analysis
showed which capabilities were closest to ready — and therefore where to start. The architecture turned
a slogan into a navigable transformation.

### Practical exercise
For a strategy you know, define 3–5 target capabilities that must become strong, the operating-model
change each implies, and the single biggest gap from today.

### Artifact produced — **Target-State Architecture** (showcaseable)
A future-state architecture (target capabilities + operating-model changes) with a current-vs-target gap
analysis — a senior consulting deliverable that proves you can design *where a business needs to go.*

> **Gate:** capability = target-state design + gap analysis · artifact = Target-State Architecture · recruiter ✅ · public ✅ · interview ✅.

## Lesson 5 · Strategic Alignment & Transformation Framing
**Competency:** `ba:business-architecture` · **Output:** Transformation Recommendation Deck (P8) · **links Sim 9**

### Objectives
- Trace every initiative to strategy through capability — the executive's funding logic.
- Frame a transformation case executives will back.
- Produce the Transformation Recommendation Deck (Portfolio P8).

### Lesson
The final business-architecture skill is **strategic alignment** — and it's the one that gets
transformations funded. Executives don't fund "modernize the platform"; they fund *"close the
capability gap that's blocking our strategy, for this return, at this risk."* Alignment means every
proposed initiative traces cleanly: **strategy → the capability it strengthens → the value stream it
improves → the measurable outcome → the investment and risk.** An initiative that can't make that trace
is a candidate to cut, no matter how popular.

Framing the **transformation case** uses the executive disciplines you built in Modules 3 and 5 — the
Executive Narrative Framework, decision-first communication — at enterprise scale. The story executives
back: *here's the strategy · here are the target capabilities · here's the heatmap showing the gap ·
here's the phased roadmap to close it · here's the investment, the outcomes, and the risks.* That is the
**Transformation Recommendation Deck** — and it's the artifact you defend in **Simulation 9 (Executive
Recommendation Board)**, the capstone of the strategic tier.

Real example (and your simulation): a business architect walks a board through exactly this chain — and
because every initiative traces to a strategic objective and a capability gap, with a credible roadmap
and numbers, the board funds a multi-year program rather than a pile of disconnected projects. The deck
is the difference between an analyst with opinions and a consultant with a fundable case.

This lesson consolidates the module into **Portfolio Artifact #8 — the Transformation Recommendation
Deck** — and feeds **Simulation 9.** Lesson → artifact → simulation → defense, at the top of the value
ladder.

### Practical exercise
Take one initiative and write its alignment trace (strategy → capability → value stream → outcome →
investment/risk). Then outline a 5-slide Transformation Recommendation Deck for it.

### Artifact produced — **Transformation Recommendation Deck (P8)** (showcaseable)
The capstone artifact of the strategic tier: strategy, target capabilities, capability-gap heatmap,
phased roadmap, investment, outcomes, and risks — **Portfolio Artifact #8**, defended in **Simulation
9.** This is the single most powerful piece in a transformation-consultant portfolio.

> **Gate:** capability = strategic alignment + transformation framing · artifact = Transformation Recommendation Deck (→ Sim 9, P8) · recruiter ✅ · public ✅ · interview ✅.
