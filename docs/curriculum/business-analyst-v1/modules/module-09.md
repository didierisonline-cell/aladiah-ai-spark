## Lesson 1 · Outcomes, Vision & Value
**Competency:** `ba:product-thinking` · **Output:** Outcome Map

### Objectives
- Make the outcomes-over-outputs shift operational, not just a slogan.
- Connect vision → outcomes → the work, so effort traces to value.
- Produce an Outcome Map.

### Lesson
Module 1 introduced outcomes over outputs; Module 9 makes it the way you *operate.* An **output** is
what you ship (a feature, a portal); an **outcome** is the measurable change it creates (more users
activate, churn drops, refund time falls). The most expensive failure in product is a **feature
factory** — a team shipping outputs efficiently while moving no outcome, busy and irrelevant. Marty
Cagan's SVPG work is blunt about it: great teams are measured by *results*, not by how much they ship.

Product thinking runs top-down from **vision** (where we're going and why it matters) to **outcomes**
(the measurable changes that get us there) to the **work** (the bets we make to move those outcomes).
The discipline is keeping the chain intact: every initiative should trace to an outcome, and every
outcome to the vision. When a stakeholder demands a feature, the product-minded BA asks *"what outcome
is this meant to create, and how will we know?"* — the same reframing that distinguishes an advisor from
an order-taker.

Real example: a team was measured on "features shipped" and shipped dozens per quarter while activation
flatlined — a textbook feature factory. Re-anchored on a single outcome ("increase new-user activation
from 22% to 35%"), they killed most of the roadmap, focused on the three bets that could move
activation, and hit the number with *less* shipped. Same team, opposite result, because they switched
from counting outputs to moving an outcome.

### Practical exercise
Take a product or initiative. Write its vision in one line, the 2–3 outcomes that would prove progress
(with metrics), and map three current pieces of work to the outcome each is supposed to move. Flag any
work that maps to none.

### Artifact produced — **Outcome Map** (showcaseable)
A vision → outcomes (with metrics) → work map, with orphaned outputs flagged — proof you can connect
strategy to delivery through value, the core of product thinking.

> **Gate:** capability = outcome-driven product thinking · artifact = Outcome Map · recruiter ✅ · public ✅ · interview ✅.

## Lesson 2 · Business-Model & North-Star Thinking
**Competency:** `ba:product-thinking` · **Output:** North-Star Definition

### Objectives
- Read a business model and where a product creates and captures value.
- Define a North-Star Metric that aligns a team without distorting behavior.
- Produce a North-Star Definition.

### Lesson
Product decisions don't happen in a vacuum — they happen inside a **business model.** The **Business
Model Canvas** (Osterwalder) is the one-page lens: value proposition, customer segments, channels,
revenue streams, cost structure, key activities. A product-minded BA reads it to understand *how the
business creates and captures value*, so product bets strengthen the model rather than fight it (recall
the omnichannel retailer in Module 8 whose model and strategy contradicted).

The sharpest alignment tool is the **North-Star Metric (NSM)** — the single measure that best captures
the value customers get *and* predicts business success (Spotify's "time spent listening," Airbnb's
"nights booked"). A good NSM aligns everyone on real value; a bad one distorts behavior (optimize "page
views" and you get clickbait). The craft: pick a metric that's a **leading indicator of customer value**,
not a vanity or purely-financial number, and pair it with **guardrail metrics** so the team can't game
it (grow the NSM *without* hurting retention or trust). Defining the right NSM is one of the most
leverage-dense decisions in product.

Real example: a marketplace optimized "transactions," and teams chased low-value transactions that
churned. Reframed around a North-Star of *"repeat purchases from satisfied buyers"* (with a satisfaction
guardrail), behavior shifted toward durable value, and revenue followed *because* the metric finally
pointed at the real thing. The metric you choose is a strategy decision in disguise.

### Practical exercise
Sketch the Business Model Canvas for a product. Propose a North-Star Metric (leading indicator of
customer value) and two guardrail metrics that prevent gaming it.

### Artifact produced — **North-Star Definition** (showcaseable)
A North-Star Metric with its rationale (why it predicts value + success) and guardrails, set in the
business-model context — an artifact that signals you think about products strategically, not feature by
feature.

> **Gate:** capability = business-model + north-star thinking · artifact = North-Star Definition · recruiter ✅ · public ✅ · interview ✅.

## Lesson 3 · Strategy: Choosing Opportunities
**Competency:** `ba:product-thinking` · **Output:** Opportunity Shortlist

### Objectives
- Distinguish strategy (choosing) from discovery (finding) — and do the choosing well.
- Use focus and sequencing to turn many possibilities into a coherent bet.
- Produce an Opportunity Shortlist.

### Lesson
This is the lesson the BA competency canon was deliberately split for: **`ba:product-discovery` is
*finding* opportunities (Module 10); `ba:product-thinking` is *choosing* them.** Strategy is, above all,
**choice** — and the hardest part is deciding what *not* to do. A strategy that pursues everything is no
strategy; it's a wish-list that spreads a team thin and moves nothing.

Good product strategy has a few hallmarks: **focus** (a small number of bets aligned to the vision and
North-Star), a **coherent logic** (these opportunities reinforce each other and the business model), and
**sequencing** (what we do first changes what becomes possible later). Roman Pichler and Cagan both
stress that strategy is the bridge *between* vision and execution — the deliberate selection of which
problems are worth solving now. The product-minded BA brings rigor to that selection: tying each
candidate opportunity to the North-Star, to the capability map (Module 8), and to evidence (Module 4),
so the shortlist is defensible rather than political.

Real example: a SaaS company had 40 "great ideas" and a stalling growth rate. The strategic move wasn't
another idea — it was *subtraction*: choosing the three opportunities that reinforced one another and
the North-Star, and explicitly parking the rest. Focus, not more activity, restarted growth. Saying no
to 37 good ideas to fund 3 great ones is the essence of product strategy — and exactly what executives
need a product-minded analyst to help them do.

### Practical exercise
List 8–10 candidate opportunities for a product. Score each against the North-Star and strategic fit,
then choose the 3 you'd fund and write one sentence on why you'd *park* the rest.

### Artifact produced — **Opportunity Shortlist** (showcaseable)
A shortlist of chosen opportunities with the rationale for inclusion *and* exclusion, tied to the
North-Star and strategy — proof you can make and defend the hardest product call: what not to do.

> **Gate:** capability = product strategy / choosing opportunities · artifact = Opportunity Shortlist · recruiter ✅ · public ✅ · interview ✅.

## Lesson 4 · Value vs Effort & Market Attractiveness
**Competency:** `ba:product-thinking` · **Output:** Prioritization Matrix

### Objectives
- Prioritize opportunities with explicit, defensible methods.
- Weigh market attractiveness, not just internal value and effort.
- Produce a Prioritization Matrix.

### Lesson
Choice needs a method, or it defaults to the loudest voice (Module 5's lesson, applied to product).
The fast heuristic is **value vs. effort** — pursue high-value/low-effort first, scrutinize
high-effort/high-value, drop low-value/high-effort. For sharper calls, **RICE** (Reach × Impact ×
Confidence ÷ Effort) forces you to be explicit about each factor, and crucially about **confidence** —
how much evidence backs the bet (linking straight to discovery, Module 10). Weighted scoring against
the North-Star keeps prioritization tied to strategy rather than enthusiasm.

The factor BAs most often miss is **market attractiveness** — the *outside* view. An opportunity can be
high-value internally and still be a bad bet if the market is small, shrinking, saturated, or
mistimed. Product thinking weighs market size, growth, competition, and timing alongside internal
value/effort, so you're not efficiently building something the market won't reward. This outside-in
discipline is what separates product *strategy* from internal feature prioritization.

Real example: two opportunities scored equally on internal value/effort. A market read showed one
served a shrinking segment with three entrenched incumbents, the other an underserved, growing segment.
Same internal score, opposite bet quality — and only the market-attractiveness lens revealed it. The
team funded the growing-market opportunity and avoided an efficient march into a dead end.

### Practical exercise
Take your Opportunity Shortlist. Score each on value vs. effort (or RICE) *and* add a market-
attractiveness read (size/growth/competition/timing). Note where the market view changes the ranking.

### Artifact produced — **Prioritization Matrix** (showcaseable)
A prioritization matrix (value/effort or RICE) augmented with market attractiveness, producing a ranked,
defensible set of bets — an artifact that shows you prioritize with evidence and an outside-in view.

> **Gate:** capability = prioritization + market attractiveness · artifact = Prioritization Matrix · recruiter ✅ · public ✅ · interview ✅.

## Lesson 5 · Assembling the Product Opportunity Assessment
**Competency:** `ba:product-thinking` · **Output:** Product Opportunity Assessment (P4) · **links Sim 6**

### Objectives
- Synthesize the module into a decision-grade opportunity assessment.
- Make a clear, evidence-backed recommendation on what to build.
- Produce the Product Opportunity Assessment (Portfolio P4).

### Lesson
Product thinking culminates in a **Product Opportunity Assessment** — the artifact that answers, with
evidence, *"is this opportunity worth pursuing, and why?"* It pulls the module together: the **outcome**
it serves and the **North-Star** it moves, the **strategic fit** (why this, why now), the **market**
read (attractiveness, competition, timing), the **value/effort/RICE** prioritization, the key
**assumptions and risks** (what would have to be true — the bridge to discovery in Module 10), and a
clear **recommendation** (pursue / park / kill). Cagan's classic "opportunity assessment" exists for
exactly this: to force a crisp go/no-go *before* a team pours months into building.

This is also where product thinking meets the boardroom: you'll defend this assessment in **Simulation 6
(Product Prioritization War Room)** against competing sponsors and finite budget — using the prioritization
method as your shield against politics. A recommendation grounded in outcome, market, and evidence
survives the war room; an opinion does not.

This lesson produces **Portfolio Artifact #4 — the Product Opportunity Assessment** — and feeds
**Simulation 6.** Lesson → artifact → simulation → defense.

### Practical exercise
For one opportunity, draft a one-page Product Opportunity Assessment: outcome/North-Star, strategic fit,
market read, prioritization, top assumptions/risks, and your recommendation.

### Artifact produced — **Product Opportunity Assessment (P4)** (showcaseable)
The module's capstone artifact: a decision-grade opportunity assessment (outcome · strategy · market ·
prioritization · assumptions/risks · recommendation) — **Portfolio Artifact #4**, defended in
**Simulation 6.** Exactly what a Product Analyst / Product Owner is hired to produce.

> **Gate:** capability = opportunity assessment + recommendation · artifact = Product Opportunity Assessment (→ Sim 6, P4) · recruiter ✅ · public ✅ · interview ✅.
