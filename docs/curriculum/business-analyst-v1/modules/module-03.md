## Lesson 1 · Stakeholder Mapping & Power Dynamics
**Competency:** `ba:stakeholders` · **Output:** Stakeholder Map & Power/Interest Grid

### Objectives
- Identify the full stakeholder set — including the non-obvious ones who can sink a project.
- Map stakeholders by power and interest, and read the influence network between them.
- Produce a Stakeholder Map a hiring manager would recognize as professional work.

### Lesson
More initiatives fail from **stakeholder neglect** than from technical problems. The classic mistake
is mapping only the people who asked for the work, then getting blindsided by someone who was never
consulted — a regulator, a middle-management layer, a downstream team whose workflow you just broke.

The workhorse tool is the **Power/Interest grid (Mendelow's matrix):** plot each stakeholder by how
much *power* they hold over the initiative and how much *interest* they have in it. The quadrants
dictate strategy: **manage closely** (high power/high interest — your sponsor and key decision-makers),
**keep satisfied** (high power/low interest — execs who can veto but don't follow detail; don't
surprise them), **keep informed** (low power/high interest — affected users who become allies or
critics), and **monitor** (low power/low interest). The error that ends careers is mis-placing a
high-power stakeholder as low-interest and letting them get surprised.

Beyond the grid, senior BAs map the **influence network** — who *really* sways whom. The org chart
says one thing; reality is that a respected principal engineer or a long-tenured ops manager may move
a decision more than their title suggests. Real example: an ERP rollout sailed through executive
approval but stalled for a year because **branch managers** — low on the org chart, high in practical
power — quietly refused to adopt it. No one had mapped them as the stakeholders who would make or
break delivery. A power/interest grid plus an influence read would have caught it on day one.

### Practical exercise
For a real initiative, list every stakeholder (push past the obvious five). Place each on a
power/interest grid. Then draw two influence arrows: who does each high-power stakeholder *listen to*?

### Artifact produced — **Stakeholder Map & Power/Interest Grid** (showcaseable)
A clean, presentable map: the grid with stakeholders placed, plus an influence-network sketch and a
one-line strategy per quadrant. A recognizable BA work sample for a portfolio.

> **Employment value:** capability = stakeholder analysis & influence reading · artifact = Stakeholder Map ·
> recruiter cares ✅ · showcaseable ✅ · interview ✅ ("walk me through how you'd map stakeholders on a transformation").

## Lesson 2 · Engagement Strategy & Sponsor Alignment
**Competency:** `ba:stakeholders` · **Output:** Stakeholder Engagement & Sponsor-Alignment Plan

### Objectives
- Turn a stakeholder map into a concrete engagement strategy (who, how, how often).
- Secure and sustain an active executive sponsor — the single biggest predictor of success.
- Produce a Sponsor-Alignment Plan.

### Lesson
A map is diagnosis; an **engagement strategy** is treatment. For each stakeholder (or group) you
decide the engagement level, the cadence, the channel, and the message — and you assign clear
accountability with a **RACI** (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) so no decision falls
through the cracks and no one is "consulted" who should have been "accountable."

The highest-leverage relationship is the **executive sponsor.** Change-management research is blunt
about this: **Prosci's studies repeatedly find active and visible executive sponsorship to be the #1
contributor to project success** — above budget, methodology, or team skill. Yet sponsors are busy and
drift. The senior BA actively *manages* the sponsor: keeps them informed in their language, arms them
to champion the work to their peers, and surfaces decisions early so the sponsor is never caught flat.

A weak sponsor is a top risk to flag, not a fact to accept. Real example: two parallel transformations
at the same firm — one with a CFO who personally opened every steering meeting and unblocked
cross-department barriers, one with a nominal sponsor who sent a delegate. The first shipped; the
second died in committee. Same company, same method — the difference was sponsor engagement, and the
BA's job is to engineer it.

### Practical exercise
Build a RACI for the key decisions on an initiative. Then write your 3-point plan to keep your sponsor
*active*: what you'll tell them, how often, and what you'll ask them to do publicly.

### Artifact produced — **Stakeholder Engagement & Sponsor-Alignment Plan** (showcaseable)
Engagement levels + cadence + channels per stakeholder, a decision RACI, and an explicit sponsor-
activation plan. A portfolio piece that signals you manage *up*, not just sideways.

> **Employment value:** capability = engagement design + sponsor management · artifact = Engagement/Sponsor plan ·
> recruiter cares ✅ · showcaseable ✅ · interview ✅ ("how do you keep an executive sponsor engaged?").

## Lesson 3 · Executive Communication & Steering Committees
**Competency:** `ba:stakeholders` · **Output:** Executive Briefing / Steering-Committee Pack · **links Sim 2**

### Objectives
- Communicate with executives in their currency: decisions, outcomes, money, risk.
- Prepare for and run a steering committee that actually makes decisions.
- Produce an Executive Briefing Pack you'll defend in Simulation 2.

### Lesson
Executives don't read; they decide. The most common way capable analysts fail at the top is **burying
the ask** — opening with background and detail, and losing the decision before they reach it. Flip it:
**lead with the decision, the recommendation, and the why** (outcome, cost, risk), then offer detail
*on demand*. The structure executives reward: *Here's the decision I need · here's my recommendation ·
here's the evidence · here are the risks and the ask.*

A **steering committee** is the engine room of a transformation — the forum where execs make
decisions, set priorities, fund work, and resolve escalations. The BA's job is to make that forum
*productive*: a tight pre-read, a small number of framed decisions (options + recommendation, not
open-ended discussion), and a clear owner and next step for every outcome. A steering committee that
leaves without decisions is a failure you could have prevented with better framing.

Real example (and your next simulation): a BA brings a discovery recommendation to a skeptical
committee — a CFO challenging the ROI, a COO challenging feasibility, Legal challenging compliance.
The analyst who survives leads with the decision and the numbers, answers objections with evidence,
and walks out with a funded plan. **Module 3 produces the pack; Simulation 2 makes you defend it.**

### Practical exercise
Take a real recommendation. Write the one-slide executive version: the decision needed, your
recommendation, three evidence bullets, the top risk, and the specific ask. Cut everything else.

### Artifact produced — **Executive Briefing / Steering-Committee Pack** (showcaseable)
A decision-first executive brief (one page/slide) + a steering-committee agenda with framed decisions.
This is the exact artifact you carry into **Simulation 2 — Executive Steering Committee.**

> **Employment value:** capability = executive communication + governance · artifact = Exec Briefing Pack ·
> recruiter cares ✅ · showcaseable ✅ · interview ✅ ("present a recommendation to our exec team").

## Lesson 4 · Political Navigation & Influence Without Authority
**Competency:** `ba:stakeholders` · **Output:** Political Landscape Map & Influence Strategy

### Objectives
- Read the political landscape: hidden agendas, competing interests, sources of resistance.
- Influence outcomes when you have no formal authority over the people involved.
- Produce a Political Landscape Map + influence strategy.

### Lesson
A BA almost never has authority over the stakeholders they must move — no one reports to you, yet you
must get a VP, an engineer, and a finance controller to align. That is **influence without authority**,
and it is the defining senior-BA skill.

Two truths. First, **organizations are political** — not in a dirty sense, but in the real sense that
people have competing interests, incentives, and unspoken agendas. The stakeholder who publicly agrees
while quietly undermining isn't evil; they have an interest you haven't surfaced (fear of headcount
cuts, a rival initiative, loss of control). The senior BA reads this landscape and addresses the
*interest*, privately, rather than fighting the public position.

Second, influence has a science. **Cialdini's principles** — reciprocity, social proof, authority,
commitment/consistency, liking, scarcity — are practical levers: build credibility (authority), show
that respected peers are on board (social proof), find genuine common ground (liking), and secure
small public commitments that people then stay consistent with. Resistance, especially from middle
managers, usually traces to **perceived loss** — of control, status, or role clarity — not
technophobia; name the real loss and you can design for adoption.

Real example: an analyst facing a blocking stakeholder didn't escalate or confront. They discovered
the VP's real concern (a pet project losing budget), reframed the proposal so both could win, and lined
up two respected peers to voice support first. The blocker became a supporter — through influence, not
authority.

### Practical exercise
For a real initiative, map each key stakeholder's *stated* position vs. their *likely underlying
interest*. For your toughest blocker, write one influence move (whose support to line up first, what
common ground to find).

### Artifact produced — **Political Landscape Map & Influence Strategy** (showcaseable)
A map of positions vs. interests, sources of resistance, and a concrete influence plan for the two
hardest stakeholders. Discreetly anonymized, it's a sophisticated portfolio/interview piece that few
junior candidates can produce.

> **Employment value:** capability = political navigation + influence without authority · artifact = Influence Strategy ·
> recruiter cares ✅ · showcaseable ✅ (anonymized) · interview ✅ ("how do you handle a stakeholder who blocks you?").

## Lesson 5 · Conflict, Negotiation & Difficult-Stakeholder Recovery
**Competency:** `ba:stakeholders` · **Output:** Stakeholder Management Plan (P7) + Conflict-Recovery Playbook

### Objectives
- Resolve stakeholder conflict by moving from positions to interests.
- Recover a relationship with a stakeholder who has lost trust in you or the project.
- Produce the consolidated Stakeholder Management Plan (Portfolio Artifact #7).

### Lesson
Conflict between stakeholders isn't a failure of analysis — it's the normal state of any change worth
making, because real change reallocates power, budget, and work. The skill is resolving it without
picking a side by seniority or splitting the difference.

The core technique comes from **Fisher & Ury's *Getting to Yes*: separate positions from interests.**
A *position* is what someone says they want ("refunds must be instant"); an *interest* is why ("I'm
tired of customer complaints"). Positions collide; interests often don't. When Customer Service
demands instant refunds and Finance demands fraud controls, their positions are opposed but their
interests (happy customers / low losses) can both be met by a confidence-scored auto-approval with a
fallback. The BA surfaces interests, finds the option that serves both, and routes the genuine
trade-offs to the right deciding authority — turning a standoff into an owned decision.

Then there's **difficult-stakeholder recovery** — the stakeholder who's been burned by a past failed
project (every enterprise has one) and now distrusts you on sight. You don't win them with logic; you
win them with **small, reliable follow-through.** Acknowledge the history, make a tiny commitment, keep
it visibly, repeat. Trust is rebuilt in increments, not arguments. Real example: a store-operations
manager who'd torpedoed two prior rollouts became a champion of the third — because the BA started by
fixing one small thing she'd complained about for years, then kept every subsequent promise.

This lesson consolidates the module into **Portfolio Artifact #7 — the Stakeholder Management Plan** —
and feeds directly into Simulation 2.

### Practical exercise
Take a real stakeholder conflict. Write each side's position and underlying interest, then propose one
option that serves both interests. Separately, draft a 3-step recovery plan for a stakeholder who
doesn't trust you.

### Artifact produced — **Stakeholder Management Plan (P7)** + **Conflict-Recovery Playbook** (showcaseable)
The capstone artifact of the module: stakeholder map, engagement + sponsor plan, political/influence
strategy, and a conflict-resolution + recovery playbook — a comprehensive, employer-grade portfolio
piece (P7) that proves you can manage people at the level the higher salary bands require.

> **Employment value:** capability = conflict resolution + relationship recovery · artifact = Stakeholder Management Plan (P7) ·
> recruiter cares ✅ · showcaseable ✅ · interview ✅ ("tell me about a time you turned around a hostile stakeholder").
