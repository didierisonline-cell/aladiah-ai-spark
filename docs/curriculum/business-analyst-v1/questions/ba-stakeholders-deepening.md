## 2. Question bank (10 new)

**Q1 · Executive influence — steering committees · Practitioner** — correct: index 1
For a BA, the primary purpose of a steering committee is to:
1. Do the BA's analysis
2. Provide executive governance, decisions, and unblock escalations
3. Replace stakeholder interviews
4. Approve the UI
**a: 1** — *A steering committee supplies executive governance — decisions, prioritization, funding, escalation. The BA prepares decisions for it and uses it to unblock and align.*

**Q2 · Executive influence — reporting · Foundational** — correct: index 0
Reporting status to executives, the BA should lead with:
1. Outcomes, risks, decisions needed, and asks — not low-level activity
2. Every task detail
3. The full backlog
4. Technical logs
**a: 0** — *Executives need a concise, decision-oriented view (outcomes/risks/decisions), not activity dumps.*

**Q3 · Executive influence — escalation · Practitioner** — correct: index 2
An impediment beyond the team's authority threatens the timeline. The BA should:
1. Wait and hope it resolves
2. Hide it to avoid alarm
3. Escalate with a clear problem, impact, options, and recommendation to the sponsor/steering committee
4. Solve it without authority
**a: 2** — *Effective escalation is structured (problem/impact/options/recommendation) and directed to the authority that can act.*

**Q4 · Political navigation — hidden agendas · Senior** — correct: index 3
Two senior stakeholders publicly agree but consistently undermine the initiative. The best approach is:
1. Ignore it
2. Escalate publicly to embarrass them
3. Proceed regardless
4. Surface the underlying concerns privately, understand the real interests, and address them with the sponsor
**a: 3** — *Misalignment behind public agreement signals unaddressed interests; the senior BA surfaces them privately and works the sponsor, not public confrontation.*

**Q5 · Political navigation — conflicting priorities · Practitioner** — correct: index 1
Two departments have conflicting priorities for the same system. The BA should:
1. Pick one arbitrarily
2. Make the conflict explicit with shared criteria and facilitate a decision at the right authority level
3. Let them fight it out
4. Build both fully
**a: 1** — *Surface the conflict transparently, apply shared prioritization criteria, and route the decision to the authority who can make it.*

**Q6 · Political navigation — resistance · Foundational** — correct: index 0
Resistance is strongest among middle managers. A likely root cause to investigate is:
1. Perceived loss of control, status, or unclear new roles under the change
2. They dislike technology
3. They are incompetent
4. Random preference
**a: 0** — *Resistance usually stems from perceived loss (control/status/role clarity), not technophobia. Diagnose the real driver to design adoption.*

**Q7 · Negotiation — scope · Foundational** — correct: index 2
In a scope negotiation, the BA's strongest position is grounded in:
1. Personal opinion
2. Whoever is loudest
3. Value, outcomes, constraints, and trade-offs tied to objectives
4. Vendor preference
**a: 2** — *Negotiating from value/outcomes/constraints/trade-offs keeps decisions defensible and aligned to objectives.*

**Q8 · Negotiation — competing priorities · Practitioner** — correct: index 3
When two sponsors both demand "top priority," the BA's most effective tool is:
1. A coin flip
2. Whoever is more senior wins automatically
3. Doing both at half speed
4. A transparent, outcome-based prioritization framework decided by a shared authority
**a: 3** — *An outcome-based framework plus the right deciding authority resolves competing "top priorities" defensibly.*

**Q9 · Consensus building — cross-functional alignment · Senior** — correct: index 1
Building enterprise consensus for a transformation, the most durable approach is:
1. Get a one-time sign-off email
2. Co-create the solution with stakeholders for ownership, and align on shared outcomes
3. Mandate it top-down only
4. Avoid the dissenters
**a: 1** — *Durable consensus comes from co-creation and shared-outcome alignment that builds ownership; mandates and avoided dissent produce fragile agreement.*

**Q10 · Consensus building — sponsorship · Practitioner** — correct: index 0
An active executive sponsor matters most because they:
1. Provide authority, remove cross-org barriers, and visibly champion the change
2. Attend demos
3. Write the requirements
4. Test the software
**a: 0** — *A strong sponsor supplies authority, unblocks cross-organizational barriers, and visibly champions adoption — the biggest predictor of transformation success.*
